AI Tutorial: Predicting GDP per Capita from Socio-Economic Indicators

In this use case, we demonstrate how data from the World Bank can be used to model and analyze the economic development of countries.

Understanding the determinants of GDP per capita is crucial for economic planning and policy evaluation. Traditional econometric models often assume linear relationships and may overlook complex interactions between indicators. By applying machine learning methods, we can uncover non-linear patterns and evaluate the combined influence of education, demographics, trade, and fiscal indicators on economic prosperity.

The primary goal is to predict the GDP per capita (constant US$) using a set of socio-economic and macroeconomic indicators that capture different aspects of development.

The selected indicators for the year 2020 include:

  • GDP per capita: The target variable: GDP divided by population, measuring economic output per person rather than personal income.

  • Primary enrollment: Gross enrollment rate in primary education.

  • Urban population %: The share of the population living in urban areas.

  • Life expectancy: Average life expectancy at birth, in years.

  • Inflation %: Annual percentage change in consumer prices.

  • Unemployment %: Share of the labor force without employment.

  • Exports % GDP: Exports of goods and services as a percentage of GDP.

  • Imports % GDP: Imports of goods and services as a percentage of GDP.

  • FDI inflows % GDP: Net foreign direct investment inflows as a percentage of GDP.

  • GDP growth %: Annual growth rate of GDP.

  • Household consumption %: Household final consumption expenditure as a percentage of GDP.

  • Tax revenue % GDP: Government tax revenue as a percentage of GDP.

The indicators were grouped into four thematic categories for interpretability:

  1. Human Capital and Demographics: Primary enrollment, Life expectancy, Urban population (%).

  2. Macroeconomic Stability: Inflation (%), Unemployment (%), GDP growth (%).

  3. External Sector: Exports (% GDP), Imports (% GDP), FDI inflows (% GDP).

  4. Domestic Demand and Fiscal Capacity: Household consumption (% GDP), Tax revenue (% GDP).

This categorization clarifies how structural, external, and policy-driven factors jointly influence income levels across countries.

After collecting the data, the indicator codes were mapped to shorter, human-readable names for easier interpretation, and rows with missing values were removed. The resulting dataset was exported to a structured CSV file for further use in machine learning experiments.

Data Overview. The complete-case country count and GDP-per-capita range must be calculated from the downloaded snapshot; neither is fixed in advance. The companion notebook defaults to a clearly labelled synthetic dataset for offline practice. That fixture does not establish empirical country findings.

The code below pulls a single cross-section of real observations (one row per country, year 2020) from the World Bank Open Data API through the wbgapi client, then renames the cryptic indicator codes to readable column names and excludes regional and income aggregates and drops countries missing a required indicator. This live branch requests observed data rather than the notebook’s synthetic fixture; what matters instead is knowing exactly which series each World Bank code refers to. The inds list is the request, and indicator_mapping_short is the decoder: NY.GDP.PCAP.KD is GDP per capita in constant US$ (the target), SE.PRM.ENRR is the gross primary-school enrollment ratio, SP.URB.TOTL.IN.ZS is the urban share of population, SP.DYN.LE00.IN is life expectancy at birth in years, FP.CPI.TOTL.ZG is consumer-price inflation, SL.UEM.TOTL.ZS is the unemployment rate, NE.EXP.GNFS.ZS and NE.IMP.GNFS.ZS are exports and imports of goods and services as a share of GDP, BX.KLT.DINV.WD.GD.ZS is net foreign-direct-investment inflows as a share of GDP, NY.GDP.MKTP.KD.ZG is the annual real GDP growth rate, NE.CON.PRVT.ZS is household final consumption as a share of GDP, and GC.TAX.TOTL.GD.ZS is government tax revenue as a share of GDP.

The labels=True argument adds economy names; skipAggs=True excludes aggregate economies so that regions are not mixed with their members. Complete-case filtering uses the target and predictors only. The retained sample depends on indicator coverage and revisions, and exclusion can bias country comparisons. Archive the downloaded data and dependency versions when reproducibility matters.

import wbgapi as wb
import pandas as pd
from pathlib import Path

Path("data").mkdir(exist_ok=True)

# Download data and simple preprocessing
inds = [
  "NY.GDP.PCAP.KD", "SE.PRM.ENRR", "SP.URB.TOTL.IN.ZS",
  "SP.DYN.LE00.IN", "FP.CPI.TOTL.ZG", "SL.UEM.TOTL.ZS",
  "NE.EXP.GNFS.ZS", "NE.IMP.GNFS.ZS", "BX.KLT.DINV.WD.GD.ZS",
  "NY.GDP.MKTP.KD.ZG", "NE.CON.PRVT.ZS", "GC.TAX.TOTL.GD.ZS"
]
df = wb.data.DataFrame(inds, time=2020, labels=True, skipAggs=True, db=2).reset_index()

indicator_mapping_short = {
  "NY.GDP.PCAP.KD": "GDP per capita",
  "SE.PRM.ENRR": "Primary enrollment",
  "SP.URB.TOTL.IN.ZS": "Urban population %",
  "SP.DYN.LE00.IN": "Life expectancy",
  "FP.CPI.TOTL.ZG": "Inflation %",
  "SL.UEM.TOTL.ZS": "Unemployment %",
  "NE.EXP.GNFS.ZS": "Exports % GDP",
  "NE.IMP.GNFS.ZS": "Imports % GDP",
  "BX.KLT.DINV.WD.GD.ZS": "FDI inflows % GDP",
  "NY.GDP.MKTP.KD.ZG": "GDP growth %",
  "NE.CON.PRVT.ZS": "Household consumption %",
  "GC.TAX.TOTL.GD.ZS": "Tax revenue % GDP"
}

# Apply mapping
df = df.rename(columns=indicator_mapping_short)
df = df.dropna(subset=list(indicator_mapping_short.values()))
print(f"Complete country observations: {len(df)}")

# Save to CSV
df.to_csv("data/gdp_per_capita_world_bank_2020.csv", index=False)

Following data preprocessing, we constructed a predictive framework to estimate GDP per capita based on the selected indicators. The modeling workflow followed a standard supervised learning pipeline comprising data splitting, model training, and performance evaluation.

Machine Learning Modeling

In this part of the study, we employ machine learning regression models to predict GDP per capita using socio-economic indicators as explanatory variables. The dataset used was preprocessed and saved as a CSV file containing 11 predictor variables and the target variable, alongside economy identifiers.

The predictors include:

  • Primary enrollment

  • Urban population (%)

  • Life expectancy

  • Inflation (%)

  • Unemployment (%)

  • Exports (% of GDP)

  • Imports (% of GDP)

  • FDI inflows (% of GDP)

  • GDP growth (%)

  • Household consumption (% of GDP)

  • Tax revenue (% of GDP)

The target variable is:

  • GDP per capita (constant US$)

The dataset was split into a training set (75%) and a test set (25%). The following regression models were implemented and evaluated:

  • Support Vector Machine (SVM)

  • Linear Regression

  • Decision Tree Regressor

  • K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)

  • Random Forest Regressor

  • XGBoost Regressor

The chosen algorithms cover a spectrum of modeling assumptions: linear (Linear Regression), non-linear tree-based (Decision Tree, Random Forest, XGBoost), instance-based (KNN), and kernel-based (SVM). This diversity enables comparison between simple interpretable models and complex ensemble methods capable of capturing non-linear interactions among predictors.

Each model was trained on the training set and evaluated on the test set. The performance was assessed using three common regression metrics:

  • Mean Absolute Error (MAE): the average absolute difference between predicted and true values.

  • Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE): the square root of the average squared differences, which penalizes larger errors.

  • Coefficient of Determination (\(R^2\)): the proportion of variance in the target variable explained by the model.

This tutorial compares six regressors and a training-mean baseline on the same split. SVM and KNN use training-fitted feature scales; SVR also standardizes the target during fitting and returns predictions in the original currency units. MAE and RMSE should be assessed relative to the income levels of the countries being evaluated. Negative test \(R^2\) means performance below the constant test-mean reference used in that metric; the separately fitted baseline reports a feasible training-mean comparison. Random seeds stabilize a run on a fixed dataset and software environment, but do not freeze live data or dependencies. This is a same-year cross-sectional prediction exercise, not future forecasting or a causal policy analysis: several predictors already contain contemporaneous GDP in their denominators. Tune models inside training folds and reserve the test sample for final evaluation.

# Machine learning part
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.svm import SVR
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeRegressor
from xgboost import XGBRegressor
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error, root_mean_squared_error
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsRegressor
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.compose import TransformedTargetRegressor
from sklearn.dummy import DummyRegressor
import pandas as pd

# Load from CSV
df = pd.read_csv("data/gdp_per_capita_world_bank_2020.csv")

X = df[[
  "Primary enrollment", "Urban population %",
  "Life expectancy", "Inflation %", "Unemployment %",
  "Exports % GDP", "Imports % GDP", "FDI inflows % GDP",
  "GDP growth %", "Household consumption %", "Tax revenue % GDP"
]]
y = df["GDP per capita"]

print(f"Dataset shape: {df.shape}")
print(f"Number of countries: {df['economy'].nunique()}")

X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=0)

models = {
  "Training mean": DummyRegressor(strategy="mean"),
  "SVM": TransformedTargetRegressor(
      regressor=make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVR()),
      transformer=StandardScaler()),
  "Linear Regression": LinearRegression(),
  "Decision Tree": DecisionTreeRegressor(random_state=0),
  "KNN": make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsRegressor()),
  "Random Forest": RandomForestRegressor(random_state=0),
  "XGBoost": XGBRegressor(random_state=0)
}

for name, model in models.items():
  model.fit(X_tr, y_tr)
  pred = model.predict(X_te)
  mae = mean_absolute_error(y_te, pred)
  rmse = root_mean_squared_error(y_te, pred)
  r2 = model.score(X_te, y_te)
  print(f"{name} - MAE: {mae:.2f}, RMSE: {rmse:.2f}, R2: {r2:.3f}")

Results Interpretation

Read the printed metrics from the particular dataset and run being evaluated. No empirical score table is asserted here because a frozen World Bank snapshot was not supplied. The notebook’s synthetic target is a noisy linear construction; rankings on that fixture demonstrate evaluation mechanics and do not establish ensemble superiority for actual economies. Tree feature importance can describe how a fitted model uses inputs, but does not identify causes of prosperity or justify a policy intervention. Compare errors across income groups and examine sensitivity to country selection and random splits before drawing conclusions.
